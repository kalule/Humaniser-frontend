Heading1 | 1.0 — Introduction
normal | Digital learning has become a strategic imperative for higher education institutions globally, reshaping how knowledge is produced, accessed, and validated across diverse geographical and socioeconomic contexts (Zeleza, 2026). The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated this transformation, exposing both the potential and the fragility of digital educational infrastructure, particularly in resource-constrained settings (Burtsev, 2021). African universities have faced acute pressure to transition to digital modalities, yet this shift has revealed systemic inequalities in technology access, institutional capacity, and student readiness (Ajani, 2026a). While digital learning platforms offer pathways to expanded access and flexible pedagogies, their adoption remains uneven across institutions and student populations, raising critical questions about equity and sustainability (Maluleke, 2025).
normal | Student adoption of digital learning technologies is influenced by interconnected factors spanning institutional, technological, and individual domains. Access to reliable internet connectivity and device affordability constitute foundational barriers in many African contexts (Lawali, 2026; Achuonye, 2026). Beyond infrastructure, institutional support systems—including technical assistance, pedagogical training, and learning management system usability—shape whether students engage meaningfully with digital tools or experience them as obstacles (Ajani, 2026a; Sangwa, 2026). Student perceptions of technology usefulness, trust in institutional systems, and prior digital literacy also mediate adoption intentions (Riyanti, 2025). However, the relative weight and interaction of these factors within the South African higher education context remain inadequately understood, particularly from students' own perspectives on how they navigate and make sense of digital learning in their everyday academic lives (Loglo, 2023).
normal | Research on digital learning adoption in sub-Saharan African universities has concentrated predominantly on quantifying access, measuring usage frequency, and identifying infrastructure deficits (Nyongesa, 2025). Fewer studies have explored the qualitative dimensions of student adoption—how learners experience digital tools, negotiate competing demands, and develop commitment to digital practices over time (Mpungose, 2025). In South Africa specifically, while some research has documented barriers to learning technology use among commerce and accounting students (Eposi, 2025), comprehensive investigation of the multifaceted factors influencing adoption across diverse student populations and institutional contexts remains limited (Yakobi, 2024). This study aims to generate in-depth qualitative understanding of the factors influencing digital learning adoption among university students in South Africa, attending to how institutional structures, technological affordances, peer influences, and individual motivations interact to shape students' engagement with digital learning practices.
Heading1 | 1.2 — Background
normal | The integration of digital technologies into university education globally has accelerated dramatically over the past two decades, with particular urgency following the COVID-19 pandemic (Zeleza, 2026). In sub-Saharan Africa, this transition has been uneven, marked by substantial investment in digital infrastructure alongside persistent challenges in translating that investment into meaningful student engagement (Mapunda, 2026). South Africa, as a middle-income country with established higher education infrastructure yet deep socioeconomic inequality, presents a distinctive case for understanding the factors that shape whether university students actually adopt and benefit from digital learning platforms.
normal | The concept of digital learning adoption refers to the deliberate, sustained embrace of technology-enabled instructional tools and environments by students as part of their formal educational experience. This process is not purely technical; adoption is mediated by individual psychological factors, institutional conditions, socioeconomic access, and pedagogical design (Lazăr, 2020). Research on technology acceptance in higher education has increasingly recognised that student intention to use digital tools depends on perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and familiarity with digital environments (Lazăr, 2020). Yet these psychological mechanisms operate within structural constraints. For disadvantaged students in South Africa, online learning experiences are shaped by interconnected barriers including inconsistent internet access, inadequate device availability, and limited preparedness for technology-mediated instruction (Nkosi, 2026).
normal | South African universities have invested substantially in learning management systems, digital libraries, and technology-enhanced classrooms over the past fifteen years. However, evidence from the broader African context reveals a critical implementation gap. In sub-Saharan African higher education more broadly, formal technology-embedded teacher training, collaborative learning models, adequate technology literacy, and reliable internet connectivity remain persistent obstacles to technology adoption for learning (Oladele, 2022). Preparedness for digital learning among both instructors and learners in sub-Saharan Africa remains inconsistent, with disparities in device access, digital proficiency, internet connectivity, and satisfaction with digital platforms (Pete, 2020). South Africa shares these regional constraints while also experiencing institution-specific variations in digital infrastructure quality and pedagogical support.
normal | The adoption of digital learning platforms is not uniform across student populations or institutional contexts. Access to technology, framed within broader social justice concerns, influences student engagement in online learning environments (Vaněček, 2026). Research on blended learning adoption demonstrates that student engagement mediates the relationship between instructional design and actual learning outcomes (Rufi'i, 2026). Yet the mechanisms through which South African students—particularly those from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds—navigate the decision to adopt digital tools, and the factors that either enable or constrain that adoption, remain incompletely mapped in the literature.
normal | Emerging evidence from other African contexts suggests multiple domains of influence. In Ghana, generative artificial intelligence literacy, self-efficacy, attitude, interest, and dependence interact to shape academic work completion among university students (Baah, 2026). In Tanzania, the behavioural, cognitive, and emotional dimensions of student engagement with digital learning platforms have begun to receive empirical attention (Mapunda, 2026). In Nigeria, electronic technology use in tertiary institutions is shaped by institutional context, user satisfaction, and metacognitive factors (Ogbonna, 2025). However, South Africa-specific research examining the particular configuration of factors influencing digital learning adoption among its university population remains limited.
normal | This gap is consequential. South African universities operate within a distinctive policy environment shaped by the National Development Plan, institutional equity mandates, and the Fourth Industrial Revolution agenda, all of which have positioned digital learning as central to access and quality (Lekena, 2026). Yet without empirical clarity on which factors—whether individual (motivation, digital literacy, self-efficacy), institutional (pedagogical support, infrastructure quality, faculty training), socioeconomic (device access, internet reliability, financial constraints), or environmental (policy alignment, ecosystem support)—most strongly influence adoption decisions among South African students, institutional strategies risk being misaligned with actual student needs and barriers.
normal | The present study addresses this gap by investigating, through qualitative enquiry, the constellation of factors that influence digital learning adoption among university students in South Africa. By foregrounding student perspectives and experiences across diverse institutional and socioeconomic contexts, this research will contribute empirically grounded understanding of adoption determinants specific to the South African higher education landscape.
Heading1 | 2.0 — Literature Review
normal | Research on technology adoption in higher education has expanded significantly over the past decade, yet the specific factors influencing digital learning adoption among university students in South Africa remain incompletely understood. The existing literature reveals several interconnected themes that shape how students engage with technology-enabled instructional environments.
normal | A foundational theme concerns the multifaceted nature of technology adoption itself. Prior scholarship demonstrates that digital learning adoption involves not merely access to infrastructure but rather sustained engagement with technology-mediated learning environments (Mapunda, 2026). Higher education institutions across sub-Saharan Africa have invested substantially in digital learning platforms, yet empirical evidence linking this investment to meaningful student engagement remains limited (Mapunda, 2026). Similarly, studies examining technology acceptance in online education contexts identify the Technology Acceptance Model as a dominant analytical framework, with recent systematic reviews of Technology Acceptance Model applications revealing barriers and opportunities across diverse educational settings (Li, 2025). In the Indian higher education context, students' adoption intentions toward digital collaboration platforms were shaped by perceived usefulness and ease of use, though effectiveness assessments remain context-dependent (Singh, 2020).
normal | A second theme addresses the pedagogical and evaluative dimensions underlying technology effectiveness. Research examining e-learning success in higher education emphasises that pedagogical, evaluative, and technological factors jointly determine platform outcomes (Triyanto, 2024). Evidence from resource-constrained university settings in East Africa indicates that students face challenges including limited learning resources and outdated pedagogical approaches, complicating the realisation of technology's potential (Dirckinck-Holmfeld, 2023). Student engagement with digital tools is further complicated by competing demands: academic stress coupled with constant digital connectivity creates tensions that influence both engagement and learning outcomes (Kakar, 2026). Institutional implementation of educational technology faces obstacles rooted in competing organisational priorities; medical teaching policy adoption in university hospitals, for instance, must navigate tensions between research, patient care, and instructional missions (Engbers, 2016).
normal | A third theme explores digital literacy and skill gaps constraining technology adoption. Systematic evidence from sub-Saharan African higher education institutions reveals significant digital literacy gaps that limit students' ability to leverage twenty-first-century skills within technology-mediated environments (Ndibalema, 2025). This gap is particularly acute as institutions undergo rapid technological transformation without corresponding support for student capability development.
normal | A fourth theme addresses institutional and contextual barriers to digital transformation. Digital transformation in African higher education represents an evolution beyond simple Information and Communication Technology integration; yet understanding how technological adoption progresses into broader institutional change remains underdeveloped (Ahouman, 2026). Universities in developing contexts face persistent infrastructure deficits and resource constraints that shape adoption patterns (Nduhura, 2026). Additionally, challenges in the administrative digitalisation of higher education systems—including coordination, governance, and change management—have been documented but require further prioritisation (Муссай, 2026).
normal | A fifth theme concerns the role of student-level factors in adoption decisions. Evidence from South African university contexts demonstrates that student engagement with digital tools is a measurable outcome enhanced through purposefully designed learning approaches (Ivala, 2013). However, research on gender and performance-based differences in online learning preferences reveals paradoxes: whilst students report positive attitudes toward digital environments, satisfaction levels vary significantly by demographic group (Mohamed, 2025). Videoconferencing fatigue emerged as an unanticipated barrier during widespread online instruction, reducing engagement despite sustained technology availability (Dacillo, 2022).
normal | Despite extensive research on technology adoption in higher education and growing scholarship on African higher education specifically, the relative weight and interplay of institutional, pedagogical, technological, and individual student factors influencing digital learning adoption in South African university contexts remains underexplored. Existing studies prioritise either institutional implementation barriers or individual acceptance models, but integrated empirical investigation of how these factors interact within the South African setting is sparse.
Heading1 | 3.1 — Problem Statement
normal | South African higher education institutions have invested substantially in digital learning infrastructure over the past decade, yet the empirical relationship between this investment and meaningful student engagement remains poorly understood. While digital learning platforms have expanded access and flexibility in teaching delivery, evidence linking platform adoption to sustained engagement with technology-mediated learning environments—the canonical definition of digital learning adoption for this study—is limited and context-dependent (Mapunda, 2026). This gap is particularly acute in the South African context, where institutional investment decisions often proceed without robust local evidence of effectiveness.
normal | The broader challenge extends beyond infrastructure deployment. In West African higher education systems, adoption of emerging technologies remains uneven, with limited comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing both initial adoption and sustained use (AGBEYINKA, 2026). Similarly, in African higher education more broadly, digital transition is frequently treated as a pedagogical or technological adjustment rather than an institutional transformation problem requiring investigation of multiple contextual factors (Sangwa, 2026). This framing obscures the complexity of adoption decisions and the diverse barriers and enablers that shape whether students and institutions actually embed digital tools into learning practice.
normal | Within South Africa specifically, empirical investigation of technology adoption in higher education remains sparse. A recent study examining chatbot technology at a rural South African university identified both the potential of artificial intelligence-assisted learning and the persistent digital divide and access barriers that constrain adoption in resource-limited contexts (Mbodila, 2026). This finding underscores that adoption cannot be understood as a uniform process: institutional setting, infrastructure availability, and student circumstances all mediate whether digital tools translate into sustained engagement. Generative artificial intelligence literacy, self-efficacy, attitude, and interest have been shown to interact in influencing academic work completion among university students in neighbouring Ghana (Baah, 2026), suggesting that psychological and motivational factors warrant investigation in the South African context as well.
normal | The consequences of this evidence gap are substantial. Without understanding which specific factors influence digital learning adoption among South African university students, institutions risk continued misalignment between technology investment and pedagogical outcomes. Decisions about platform selection, implementation timing, and support structures remain largely reactive rather than informed by local evidence. Furthermore, the absence of context-specific research perpetuates assumptions drawn from higher-income settings, potentially overlooking the distinctive institutional, infrastructural, and socioeconomic conditions that shape adoption in South Africa.
normal | This study therefore seeks to investigate the factors influencing digital learning adoption among university students in South Africa, using qualitative methods to generate context-grounded understanding of the barriers, enablers, and processes through which students engage with technology-mediated learning environments.
Heading1 | 3.2 — Justification
normal | Digital learning adoption has become a critical priority for South African universities, yet empirical understanding of the specific factors driving or hindering this adoption among students remains fragmented. While institutions have invested substantially in technology infrastructure and online platforms, the translation of these investments into sustained student engagement with technology-mediated learning environments depends on contextual factors that remain inadequately documented in the South African higher education context. This study is timely because it addresses a documented gap between infrastructure provision and meaningful educational outcomes, a disparity that policy and practice continue to underestimate.
normal | The urgency is underscored by broader regional and institutional challenges. Policy development for technology-enabled education across African universities increasingly requires evidence grounded in local conditions rather than models derived from high-income contexts (Kaliisa, 2019). South African institutions operate within constrained resource environments while simultaneously managing post-pandemic recovery and expectations for digital competence among students entering the workforce. Understanding the factors influencing digital learning adoption in this setting generates actionable insights for institutional leaders, academic staff, and technology planners who must allocate limited resources strategically (Ntorukiri, 2022). Furthermore, institutional management policies and governance structures shape the feasibility and sustainability of digital learning innovation (Hussaini, 2025), yet little is known about how South African students experience or navigate these policy environments in practice.
normal | The study will benefit multiple stakeholder groups. For institutional practitioners—including learning and teaching coordinators, educational technologists, and faculty—empirical evidence of adoption barriers and enablers offers direct guidance for redesigning support services and technology implementation strategies. Policymakers within institutions and provincial higher education departments can use findings to align strategic technology investments with documented student needs and contextual constraints. For academic researchers and scholars in education technology, this work contributes to a geographically and culturally specific evidence base, addressing the acknowledged shortage of African-focused empirical research on technology adoption in higher education. For students themselves, clarifying the factors that influence meaningful engagement with digital learning platforms has potential to improve their learning experiences and academic outcomes.
normal | This research aligns with national and international imperatives. South Africa's National Development Plan and higher education sector strategies emphasize technological capability and equitable access to quality learning; documenting student-level adoption factors directly informs implementation of these commitments (Salmon, 2025). Globally, the Sustainable Development Goal 4 (Quality Education) explicitly targets inclusive and equitable learning opportunities, and understanding technology adoption barriers in a middle-income African context contributes evidence toward that goal. A qualitative methodology is justified because the research seeks to uncover the nuanced, contextual, and often tacit factors shaping student behaviour and decision-making around technology use—dimensions that quantitative measurement alone cannot adequately capture. Qualitative inquiry allows students to articulate their own explanations for adoption or non-adoption, revealing institutional, social, technical, and individual influences that may be invisible to survey instruments.
Heading1 | 3.3 — Conceptual Framework
normal | This study adopts the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) as its primary conceptual framework, extended to encompass the contextual specificities of South African higher education. The UTAUT framework posits that technology adoption is determined by four core constructs: performance expectancy (the degree to which an individual believes a technology will enhance job or learning performance), effort expectancy (the perceived ease of use), social influence (the extent to which an individual perceives that important others believe they should use the technology), and facilitating conditions (the perceived availability of organisational and technical support for technology use) (Gupta, 2026). This framework has been extensively applied in educational technology research across diverse geographical contexts, yet its construct relationships and validity assumptions have been developed primarily in North American and European settings (Mbona, 2026). Consequently, the lived experiences and decision-making processes of South African university students may not be fully captured by the framework's original formulation.
normal | The study recognises that digital learning adoption—operationalised in this research as sustained engagement with technology-mediated learning environments—is shaped by the interaction of individual-level factors, institutional structures, and socioeconomic constraints specific to the South African context. Prior research identifies perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, social influence, and facilitating conditions as key constructs affecting technology adoption (Ajani, 2026b), yet the relative weight and interaction of these factors in driving digital learning adoption among South African students has not been systematically documented. Extensions to the UTAUT model, including UTAUT2, have incorporated additional constructs such as hedonic motivation and habit formation (Jacques, 2025), reflecting recognition that adoption decisions involve both utilitarian and affective dimensions. However, the applicability of these extended models to the specific context of South African university students remains underexplored in the published literature.
normal | Institutional, infrastructural, and socioeconomic barriers—including inconsistent internet access, limited device ownership, and variable institutional digital readiness—have been documented as constraints on digital learning adoption in sub-Saharan African universities (Mabotja, 2025; Lekota, 2026). Research examining learning management system adoption in South African rural university contexts has identified that students recognise the value of digital platforms yet encounter significant structural obstacles to sustained engagement (Ajani, 2026b). Similarly, studies of e-learning adoption across African universities reveal that students face substantial barriers including poor digital infrastructure and inadequate institutional support (Umar, 2026). The specific mechanisms through which these structural constraints influence student adoption decisions, however, remain partially understood and warrant qualitative investigation grounded in participants' own accounts.
normal | Gender, generational, and sociodemographic factors also shape technology adoption patterns. Research on e-learning tool adoption in South African universities has documented differential adoption preferences between male and female students (Garutsa, 2026), and studies of smartphone application adoption among Generation Y university students in South Africa have identified brand identity and convenience as significant adoption drivers (Kasavan, 2025). These findings suggest that adoption is not a uniform process but rather varies according to students' social positioning and prior experiences with technology. Organisational culture and institutional messaging further influence adoption; research in African university contexts has demonstrated that institutional culture significantly shapes academic outcomes and, by extension, student engagement with institutional technologies (Otieno, 2026).
normal | The framework guiding this study integrates UTAUT's core constructs with attention to the contextual embeddedness of adoption decisions. Rather than treating adoption as a discrete, individual choice, the study conceptualises it as an ongoing process shaped by the interaction between students' perceptions of technology (performance expectancy, effort expectancy), their social networks and institutional environments (social influence, facilitating conditions), and the structural realities of their learning contexts. This interpretive lens acknowledges that students' accounts of their adoption decisions will reflect not only rational assessments of technology utility but also their navigation of resource constraints, institutional cultures, and peer influences specific to South African higher education. Theoretical sampling will be guided by the framework's core constructs, ensuring that data collection captures variation in how students experience and interpret each dimension of adoption. Data interpretation will remain grounded in participants' own language and reasoning rather than imposing the framework's categories a priori, allowing emergent patterns to refine and extend the framework's applicability to this context. The study's claims to transferability will rest on the richness and specificity of the contextual description provided, enabling readers in other South African or comparable sub-Saharan African settings to assess the relevance of findings to their own contexts.
Heading1 | 4.1 — Research Questions
normal | RQ1: What specific contextual factors within South African higher education institutions influence students' initial adoption of digital learning platforms?
normal | RQ2: To what extent do institutional factors—including infrastructure availability, technical support, and pedagogical integration—shape sustained engagement with technology-mediated learning environments?
normal | RQ3: How do students' socioeconomic circumstances, prior technology exposure, and digital literacy levels interact to influence digital learning adoption in a resource-constrained setting?
normal | RQ4: What role does peer influence and institutional social norms play in students' decisions to adopt and maintain engagement with digital learning modalities?
normal | RQ5: How do students themselves characterize the barriers and enabling conditions for meaningful digital learning adoption within their institutional and personal contexts?
normal | These five research questions collectively address the empirical gap identified in the literature by directing inquiry toward the specific, context-dependent factors that influence digital learning adoption among South African university students. Rather than treating adoption as a uniform phenomenon driven solely by technological capability, the questions recognize that meaningful engagement with technology-mediated learning environments emerges from the interplay of institutional infrastructure, individual circumstances, social dynamics, and students' own interpretations of their learning needs. RQ1 and RQ2 establish the institutional and infrastructural dimensions; RQ3 addresses the individual and socioeconomic specificity of the South African context; RQ4 incorporates social and normative influences; and RQ5 grounds the inquiry in students' lived experience and agency. Together, these questions enable a qualitative exploration of why digital learning adoption remains uneven across South African universities despite substantial institutional investment, and how sustained engagement with technology-mediated learning is actually constructed in practice.
Heading1 | 4.2 — General Objective
normal | The general objective of this study is to identify and examine the specific contextual factors influencing digital learning adoption among university students in South Africa. Digital learning adoption is defined as sustained engagement with technology-mediated learning environments; this study seeks to understand the institutional, infrastructural, socioeconomic, and social mechanisms that shape whether students develop and maintain this engagement across their academic experience.
normal | This objective is academically significant because it addresses a documented gap in empirical research on technology adoption in South African higher education. While substantial institutional investment in digital learning platforms has occurred over the past decade, the relationship between this investment and meaningful student engagement remains incompletely understood and context-dependent. South Africa's position as a middle-income country with established higher education infrastructure yet persistent socioeconomic inequality creates a distinctive environment in which technology adoption operates unevenly across student populations—a phenomenon inadequately captured by generic adoption frameworks developed in resource-rich contexts. By centering the South African context and its specific constraints, this study generates evidence about how students navigate digital learning within real conditions of variable infrastructure, technical support, digital literacy, and institutional social norms.
normal | The objective is also practically significant for institutional leadership and educational planners. Understanding which factors enable or obstruct digital learning adoption allows university administrators to deploy infrastructure investment and support services more effectively, targeting interventions toward the barriers students themselves identify. A qualitative inquiry into student experiences, institutional conditions, and socioeconomic interactions yields actionable intelligence about where institutional effort yields substantive change in engagement—and where investment without attention to students' lived circumstances produces platforms without meaningful adoption. This knowledge supports evidence-informed policy and resource allocation in South African higher education.

Collectively, they move beyond identifying adoption as a binary outcome to examining the institutional structures (SO1), individual circumstances (SO2), social mechanisms (SO3), and subjective experiences (SO4) that sustain or undermine engagement with technology-mediated learning environments. SO5 integrates these findings, recognizing that adoption is neither determined by infrastructure alone nor by student characteristics in isolation, but emerges from the interaction of multiple contextual layers specific to the South African higher education setting. This structured decomposition enables the qualitative inquiry to generate evidence directly addressable to university administrators seeking evidence-informed deployment of digital platforms and student support services.
Heading1 | 5.1 — Study Area
normal | This study examines digital learning adoption among university students in South Africa, a context characterised by significant infrastructure disparities and uneven access to technology-mediated learning environments. South Africa's higher education sector has invested substantially in digital platforms and online learning infrastructure, yet empirical understanding of the specific contextual factors shaping student adoption remains fragmented and setting-dependent. The study area encompasses public and private universities across South Africa's provinces, focusing on undergraduate and postgraduate students engaged with institutional learning management systems and technology-enabled teaching.
normal | South Africa presents a particularly relevant setting for investigating digital learning adoption because the country exhibits marked socioeconomic inequality and digital access disparities that directly influence how students engage with technology-mediated learning. Research examining online learning experiences of disadvantaged students in South African higher education has identified that students' adoption trajectories are shaped by interconnected barriers spanning infrastructure, socioeconomic circumstances, and institutional support (Nkosi, 2026). These contextual constraints—including inconsistent broadband availability, device ownership limitations, and variable institutional capacity to support technology integration—create conditions where digital learning adoption cannot be understood through individual factors alone but must be examined within the institutional and socioeconomic landscape in which students operate.
normal | The selection of South African universities as the study setting is justified by the presence of documented gaps between institutional technology investment and empirical evidence of meaningful student engagement. Prior research on technology adoption in higher education has identified that acceptance and rejection of digital tools among students varies substantially and requires deeper exploration of contextual factors (Valencia-Arías, 2024). Additionally, research on electronic technology use in tertiary institutions within comparable African contexts has demonstrated that adoption patterns are mediated by institutional context and user satisfaction with educational technology systems (Ogbonna, 2025). South Africa's position as a middle-income country with both advanced institutional capacity in some universities and severe resource constraints in others makes it an exemplary setting for understanding how individual, institutional, and contextual factors interact to shape digital learning adoption trajectories across diverse student populations.
Heading1 | 5.2 — Study Design
normal | This study adopts a phenomenological qualitative research design to explore the lived experiences and contextual interpretations of university students regarding digital learning adoption in South Africa. Phenomenology is the appropriate tradition for this inquiry because it enables systematic examination of how students subjectively experience and construct meaning from the factors influencing their sustained engagement with technology-mediated learning environments, rather than testing predetermined hypotheses about adoption determinants (Ajani, 2026b). The research questions ask not whether students adopt digital learning, but how they interpret institutional, social, and personal circumstances that enable or constrain that adoption—a fundamentally interpretive rather than positivist aim.
normal | The study is grounded in an interpretivist epistemological stance, recognizing that understanding digital learning adoption requires engagement with how students construct meaning from their lived experience with technology, institutional contexts, and pedagogical practices (Ajani, 2026b). Technology adoption cannot be meaningfully reduced to objective measurement of individual traits or behavioural indicators; instead, it emerges through students' negotiation of infrastructure, resources, peer influence, and institutional norms—all of which are socially contingent and context-dependent. This interpretive orientation aligns directly with the research gap, which calls for understanding adoption within South Africa's specific landscape of established digital infrastructure intersecting with persistent socioeconomic inequality and uneven technology exposure.
normal | Purposive sampling with maximum variation will ensure that the phenomenon of digital learning adoption is captured across students with differing socioeconomic backgrounds, disciplinary contexts, prior technology exposure, and engagement with rural versus urban institutional contexts (Ajani, 2026b; Garutsa, 2026). Semi-structured interviews will serve as the primary data collection method, allowing participants to articulate their experiences and reasoning in their own terms while enabling the researcher to probe emerging themes and contradictions in real time (Ajani, 2026b). Thematic analysis aligned with phenomenological principles will systematically identify and organize the lived experiences and meanings students attribute to factors influencing adoption while maintaining attention to the structural and contextual conditions enabling or constraining that engagement. Credibility will be strengthened through prolonged engagement with interview data and reflexive analysis of how the researcher's own assumptions about technology adoption might shape interpretation of participants' accounts (Garutsa, 2026).
Heading1 | 5.3 — Study Population
normal | The target population for this study comprises enrolled undergraduate and postgraduate students at South African universities who have been exposed to digital learning platforms as part of their formal curriculum. South African university students represent a contextually significant population for investigating digital learning adoption because they operate within a higher education system characterized by substantial institutional technology investment yet uneven student engagement (Da Silva, 2025). This population was selected because understanding adoption trajectories within university settings directly addresses the research gap: while South African institutions have deployed digital learning infrastructure, empirical evidence linking these investments to meaningful student engagement and adoption remains limited and context-dependent.
normal | The accessible study population consists of currently enrolled students at participating South African universities across multiple institutions and academic disciplines. The study will recruit students stratified by academic level (undergraduate and postgraduate), to enable investigation of variation in adoption trajectories across student populations (Research Question 5). Within this accessible population, purposive sampling will prioritize students representing diverse socioeconomic circumstances and baseline digital literacy levels, since the research design requires participants capable of reflecting on how these dimensions interact with adoption decisions (Research Question 3). This stratified approach ensures the study captures heterogeneity in both institutional contexts and individual circumstances rather than treating adoption as uniform across the student body.
normal | The distinction between target and accessible populations reflects a practical reality: while the target population encompasses all South African university students engaged with digital learning, the accessible population comprises those at participating institutions whose enrolment records and teaching schedules enable contact and interview scheduling during the data-collection window. Students will be included if they have completed at least one semester of study in a technology-mediated learning environment; this minimum exposure ensures participants possess sufficient experience to articulate factors influencing adoption decisions. The study population is appropriate for answering the research questions because it directly experiences the institutional, infrastructural, social, and individual circumstances that the five specific objectives are designed to examine.
Heading1 | 5.4 — Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria
normal | Participants will be included if they are currently enrolled as full-time or part-time students at a South African university, have been actively engaged with at least one digital learning platform or learning management system for a minimum of one semester, and are able to articulate their experiences and perspectives on factors influencing their adoption decisions. This engagement requirement ensures that participants possess direct, lived experience with the phenomenon under study and can provide information-rich accounts of the contextual factors shaping digital learning adoption (Komba, 2024). The inclusion of students across varying disciplinary contexts, socioeconomic backgrounds, and institutional settings—including both rural and urban universities—reflects the study's commitment to maximum variation sampling, which strengthens the depth and breadth of understanding regarding how adoption operates unevenly across student populations.
normal | Participants will be excluded if they have not used any digital learning tools within the university context, have been enrolled for fewer than one semester, or are unable to communicate their experiences in English or the institution's primary language of instruction. Students who are exclusively passive consumers of pre-recorded content without engagement in interactive or collaborative digital learning activities will also be excluded, as their experiences may not adequately reflect the active adoption processes central to this study's research questions. Additionally, students who are employed as teaching assistants or technology support staff within their institution will be excluded to avoid perspectives shaped primarily by institutional roles rather than student experiences of adoption barriers and enabling conditions.
normal | These criteria serve multiple purposes within the qualitative research design. Transparent reporting of eligibility criteria enhances the trustworthiness of qualitative research by enabling readers to assess whether findings are likely applicable to their own contexts and populations (Senanayake, 2025). The criteria are designed to reduce confounding variation by removing cases whose characteristics diverge substantially from the core phenomenon, thereby strengthening the coherence and interpretability of findings (Senanayake, 2025). Furthermore, the inclusion of students from disadvantaged socioeconomic circumstances and rural institutional contexts directly addresses the study's focus on how resource constraints and infrastructure variability shape adoption patterns (Ajani, 2026b; Nkosi, 2026), ensuring that the sample captures the heterogeneous experiences documented in South African higher education contexts.
Heading1 | 5.5 — Sample Size
normal | This study anticipates a purposive sample of approximately 30–40 university students from South African institutions. This range reflects the qualitative logic of data saturation rather than statistical power calculation, which would be inappropriate for qualitative research on factors influencing digital learning adoption. Data saturation—the point at which additional interviews yield no new themes, codes, or insights about the mechanisms and contextual factors shaping students' engagement with technology-mediated learning environments—serves as the adequacy standard guiding final sample size (Ajani, 2026b). The anticipated range acknowledges that saturation may be reached earlier or later depending on the thematic complexity and diversity of participant experiences encountered during analysis.
normal | Sample adequacy in this phenomenological study is determined by information power rather than by numerical conventions alone. Information power holds that a smaller sample composed of participants with high relevance to the research questions—in this case, students actively engaging with or resisting digital learning platforms across differing socioeconomic backgrounds and institutional contexts—can yield sufficient depth to address the study's aims (Ajani, 2026b). Purposive sampling with maximum variation, as established in the methodology, ensures that participants are selected because they have direct experience with digital learning adoption decisions and can speak meaningfully to the phenomenon under study (Ajani, 2026b; Komba, 2024).
normal | Methodological adequacy requires that sample composition captures the range of factors influencing adoption rather than prioritises absolute numbers. Explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria will specify participants' demonstrated engagement with digital learning platforms, level of study (first-year through final-year undergraduate or postgraduate), and institutional location (rural versus urban South African university contexts) to ensure information-richness and transparency (Ajani, 2026b). The final sample size will be adjusted iteratively as data collection proceeds toward saturation; if thematic redundancy is evident after 25 interviews, collection will conclude; if new dimensions of experience emerge after 35 interviews, collection will continue until no substantive new insights appear.
Heading1 | 5.6 — Sampling Procedure
normal | This study employs purposive sampling with maximum variation to identify information-rich participants capable of articulating their lived experiences of digital learning adoption (Komba, 2024). Purposive sampling is appropriate for this qualitative inquiry because understanding factors influencing digital learning adoption requires participants with direct and varied experience of the phenomenon, which probability sampling cannot guarantee in the specialised population of university students. Maximum variation sampling ensures that participants differ systematically across dimensions likely to influence adoption experiences, including socioeconomic background, disciplinary context, year of study, prior digital technology exposure, and institutional location (rural versus urban).
normal | Participant identification will proceed through criterion-based selection applied to multiple recruitment channels. Inclusion criteria are: enrolled full-time status at a South African university; active engagement (at least three months' exposure) with institution-provided digital learning platforms; and fluency in English sufficient for interview participation. Exclusion criteria will screen out students unable to provide informed consent and those with no documented access to digital learning environments. Recruitment will occur through academic departments, student representative organisations, university learning support services, and digital platforms such as institutional learning management systems and student email lists, thereby reducing sampling bias by reaching diverse populations with varying levels of engagement (Matarirano, 2025).

Heading1 | 5.7 — Study Variables
normal | Digital learning adoption in this study is operationalised across behavioural and subjective dimensions—not as a binary acceptance or rejection, but as a spectrum of sustained engagement reflecting how students integrate technology into their learning practices over time (Ubaidah, 2026). A phenomenological qualitative approach captures how students themselves construct meaning around the factors shaping this adoption, rather than reducing it to a single metric or binary outcome.
normal | The study distinguishes adoption as a distinct construct from initial technology acceptance, recognising that sustained engagement operates differently across students with varying socioeconomic backgrounds, prior technology exposure, and institutional contexts [CITATION NEEDED]. Student-centred factors—including perceived usefulness, self-efficacy, and attitude towards digital tools—are operationalised separately from institutional factors such as infrastructure quality and technical support provision, and from contextual influences including socioeconomic access and prior exposure. This multi-level operationalisation reflects the reality that meaningful adoption emerges from interaction across individual, institutional, and contextual domains rather than from any single causal pathway.
normal | Interview narratives serve as the primary data source for all constructs, with thematic analysis applied reflexively to identify patterns in how students articulate experiences of adoption factors. Contextual influences—including rural versus urban institution location and disciplinary variation—are documented throughout the analysis to clarify how findings reflect the specific South African higher education setting rather than universal adoption mechanisms.

Heading1 | 5.9 — Quality Assurance

Memos attached to coded segments will document analytical decisions, emerging interpretations, and reflexive notes about researcher assumptions, supporting the phenomenological principle of transparent meaning-making. A second coder will independently code two randomly selected transcripts (approximately 15% of the dataset), with discrepancies resolved through discussion and comparison using NVivo's coding comparison query function to ensure consistency across the coding scheme.

Access to raw transcripts and coded data will be restricted to the principal investigator and supervising faculty; the de-identified codebook may be shared with the research team for training and audit purposes only.

Heading1 | 5.12 — Study Limitations
normal | A phenomenological qualitative design prioritises depth of understanding over breadth of representation. Accordingly, this study does not aim to generalise findings statistically to the entire South African university student population; instead, it seeks to illuminate the lived experiences and contextual meanings that shape digital learning adoption within particular institutional and socioeconomic settings. Transferability of findings will depend on the degree to which readers and policymakers recognise analytic patterns in their own contexts rather than on sampling representativeness. The purposive sampling strategy with maximum variation, whilst essential for capturing heterogeneity in socioeconomic circumstances, digital literacy, and geographic location, remains bounded to approximately 35–45 students across participating institutions. This sample size permits detailed thematic analysis but may not exhaust the full spectrum of adoption experiences or capture adoption patterns unique to institution types, disciplines, or student cohorts not well-represented in recruitment. Interpretation must therefore remain cautious about whether emergent themes reflect near-universal structural features of South African higher education or are artefacts of who participated.
normal | Semi-structured interviews depend on participant willingness and ability to articulate subjective experience verbally. Students experiencing acute digital exclusion, language barriers when expressing nuanced technical or academic concepts, or reluctance to disclose socioeconomic disadvantage may provide limited or guarded accounts. This limitation will be mitigated through careful interview training, use of plain language, and explicit assurance of confidentiality; however, some experiences may remain partially inaccessible. Additionally, interviews capture retrospective accounts and stated perceptions of adoption factors rather than real-time observation of technology use. Students may rationalise past decisions or unknowingly conflate influences; interview narratives reflect how they make sense of adoption, not necessarily the mechanisms driving it.
normal | Researcher positionality represents a final boundary. The research team's own prior exposure to digital platforms, institutional affiliations, and assumptions about technology adoption may shape how questions are framed, which probes are pursued, and how thematic patterns are interpreted. Reflexive practice—documented through researcher journals and peer debriefing during analysis—will make these assumptions explicit and help prevent uncritical projection onto participant accounts. Nonetheless, this influence cannot be wholly eliminated; readers should consider researcher perspective when weighing the credibility and relevance of findings to their contexts.
